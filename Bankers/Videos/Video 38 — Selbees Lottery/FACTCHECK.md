# Fact-check: "Jerry and Marge Selbee" reel script (Bankers)

Checked 2026-11-25. The 2022 film was not used as a source.

**Main source families**
- **[CBS]** CBS News / 60 Minutes, "How a retired couple found lottery odds in their favor", correspondent Jon Wertheim, published 2019-01-27 (full transcript on page). https://www.cbsnews.com/news/jerry-and-marge-selbee-how-a-retired-couple-won-millions-using-a-lottery-loophole-60-minutes/
- **[HL]** Jason Fagone, "Jerry and Marge Go Large" (page title "The Lottery Hackers"), HuffPost Highline, 2018-03-01. https://highline.huffingtonpost.com/articles/en/lotto-winners/
- **[TREAS]** Office of the Massachusetts State Treasurer, "Comment on Report by Office of the Inspector General Re: Massachusetts State Lottery Cash WinFall Game" (July 2012; summarises the OIG's findings). Massachusetts State Library archive: https://archives.lib.state.ma.us/server/api/core/bitstreams/ea1512d5-cb14-4881-a5c1-67fc2cfabcb5/content
- **[GLOBE-OCT11]** Andrea Estes, "Lottery officials helped high rollers see windfalls", Boston Globe, 2011-10-16 (reprint PDF): https://www.stoppredatorygambling.org/wp-content/uploads/2012/12/Mass.-Lottery-Officials-Helped-High-Rollers-See-Windfalls.pdf

The IG report itself (Gregory W. Sullivan, July 2012; it is listed as "Letter to State Treasurer Steven Grossman regarding the Lottery, July 2012" at https://www.mass.gov/lists/oig-2012-reports-and-recommendations) could not be fetched because mass.gov refused the automated request (HTTP 403), and I did not retry. The IG's findings below are taken from the Treasurer's official comment [TREAS], from CBS (Sullivan interview) and from HL. HL dates the IG report 2012-07-27. The original Boston Globe story of 2011-07-31 ("A game with a windfall for a knowing few", Andrea Estes and Scott Allen) is no longer online at boston.com (404) and is not in the Wayback Machine. Its date, headline and byline are confirmed by HL and its content by CBS (Scott Allen interview) and GLOBE-OCT11.

**One source family per figure:** the script's figures (1,900, three minutes, $3,600/$6,300, six years, 900 miles, $600,000, $120M, $26M, ~$8M) are all CBS figures. HL often gives different numbers (700 miles, $3,400, five years, nearly $27M, $7.75M). Don't mix them in one line.

## Line 1
> "A retired couple from a town of 1,900 people beat the lottery. Legally. For nine years."

**VERDICT: correct**

- CBS, 2019-01-27: "For years, high school sweethearts Jerry and Marge Selbee lived a quiet life in Evart, Michigan, population 1900."
- CBS: "In total, their unlikely homegrown company grossed more than $26 million from nine years of playing the lottery."
- HL, 2018-03-01: "Evart, Michigan: 1,903 residents" and "they had grossed nearly $27 million from nine years of playing the lottery in two states."
- Context: the US Census counted Evart at 1,738 (2000), 1,903 (2010) and 1,742 (2020), per https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evart,_Michigan citing the Census Bureau. "1,900" matches the 2010 count, which falls inside the story's 2003–2012 period.
- The game ran from 2003 to January 2012. HL: "The last time Jerry and Marge played Cash WinFall was in January 2012." "Nine years" is the figure both CBS and HL use. (HL's "Final Tally" box says "Over eight years", which is an internal inconsistency. Strictly, 2003 to January 2012 is about 8.5 years.)
- "Legally" is supported by the IG findings (see Line 8). Note that CBS's own framing is that they "didn't so much as beat the lottery odds as they figured them out", but "beat the lottery" is a fair hook.

No change needed.

## Line 2
> "In 2003, Jerry Selbee, who had just sold his convenience store in Evart, Michigan, picked up a brochure for a new game called Winfall. It took him three minutes to find the flaw."

**VERDICT: needs fix ("had just sold")**

- HL, 2018-03-01: "Finally, in 2000, Jerry and Marge decided it was time to retire. … Sometimes he'd stop by the Corner Store too, chatting with the new owners … It was on one of these mornings at the Corner Store, in 2003, that Jerry saw the brochure for the new lottery game." This puts the sale about three years before 2003, not "just" before.
- CBS, 2019-01-27: "Jon Wertheim: How long did you have the store? Jerry Selbee: 17 years. … Jon Wertheim: Why'd you decide to sell it? Jerry Selbee: I was 62. Marge was 63." … "But one morning in 2003, Jerry happened to walk back into the corner store and spotted a brochure for a brand new lottery game called Winfall."
- **Ages:** 62 and 63 were their ages when they **sold the store**, not in 2003. HL: "at age 64, Jerry found himself contemplating … the lottery." Don't put 62/63 against 2003. (HL also quotes Jerry, about May 2005, as saying "I was 68 years old", which is inconsistent with "64" in 2003. Avoid ages altogether.)
- **Store type:** CBS says "ran a local convenience store on Main Street". HL says "a convenience store" called the Corner Store, and Jerry had "gathered data for 32 'party stores'" before buying it in 1984. "Convenience store" is correct. On the 17 years: CBS has Jerry saying 17, while HL's 1984–2000 is about 16 ("more than 15 years").
- **Three minutes:** CBS: "Jerry Selbee: No, no, not at all. Three minutes. Jon Wertheim: Three minutes and you found the loophole in the state lottery?" This is Jerry's own claim. HL says "within minutes".
- **"Flaw":** Jerry called it "a special feature" and CBS calls it "the loophole". For the Massachusetts version, the IG found "The game's design was not flawed, and it worked as intended" [TREAS]. "Loophole" (CBS's word) is more neutral than "flaw".
- **"New game called Winfall":** correct. CBS: "a brand new lottery game called Winfall".

**Proposed (31 words vs 33):**
"In 2003, Jerry Selbee, retired from his convenience store in Evart, Michigan, picked up a brochure for a new game called Winfall. It took him three minutes to spot the loophole."

## Line 3
> "When the jackpot reached five million dollars and nobody won it, the money didn't roll over. It rolled down, to everyone who matched three, four or five numbers. On those days, if you bought enough tickets, the math was on your side."

**VERDICT: correct**

- CBS, 2019-01-27: "That feature was called a 'Rolldown', and the lottery announced when it was coming. Unlike the Mega Millions games … where the jackpot keeps building until someone hits all six numbers …, in Winfall, if the jackpot reached $5 million, and no one matched all six numbers, all the money 'rolled down' to the lower-tier prize winners, dramatically boosting the payouts of those who matched five, four or three numbers."
- HL, 2018-03-01 (more precise): "If nobody won the jackpot for a while, and the jackpot climbed above $5 million, there was a roll-down, which meant that on the next drawing, as long as there was no six-number winner, the jackpot cash flowed to the lesser tiers of winners". HL also notes that two-number matches won prizes.
- "Nobody won it" means nobody matched all six numbers, which is what CBS says. "Didn't roll over" is a fair contrast with ordinary rollover jackpots.
- "If you bought enough tickets": GLOBE-OCT11, 2011-10-16: "the odds of winning lower-tier prizes are relatively good - so good that statisticians say if groups bet enough money they will win more than they lose."
- **Massachusetts was different:** Cash WinFall rolled down at **$2 million**, not $5M. TREAS: "designed to 'roll-down' the betting pool to lower-tier prizes once the pool reached $2 million". GLOBE-OCT11: "Under Cash WinFall rules, when the jackpot hits $2 million and no one wins, the prize money 'rolls down'". HL: "the jackpot rolled down when it hit $2 million, not $5 million." Line 3 is about the Michigan game, so it stays correct. Just don't apply $5M to Massachusetts.

No change needed.

## Line 4
> "Jerry tested it. Three thousand six hundred dollars in. Six thousand three hundred back."

**VERDICT: correct per CBS. Note the conflicting HL account.**

- CBS, 2019-01-27: "When a rolldown was announced, Jerry sprang into action. He bought $3,600 in Winfall tickets and won $6,300. Then he bet $8,000 and nearly doubled it."
- HL, 2018-03-01, gives a different sequence: "The first time Jerry played, at a convenience store in Mesick, he lost money. … he spent $2,200 … His winnings added up to $2,150, slightly less than he had spent on the tickets." Then: "During the next roll-down week, he returned to Mesick and made a larger bet, purchasing $3,400 in Winfall tickets. … This time he won $6,300". Then: "he bet even more on the next roll-down, $8,000, and won $15,700".
- On the brief's question about a $2,200 test returning about $1,900: HL's figure is $2,150, not $1,900. The "$1,900" in CBS is Jerry's hypothetical mental arithmetic on $1,100 ("So I got $1,100 invested and I've got a $1,900 return"). It was not a real bet.
- So $3,600→$6,300 is CBS's figure and is consistent within CBS. HL says the first real-money test was a small loss of $2,200→$2,150, and that the winning bet was $3,400.

The line can stay as written, since it is CBS-consistent. **Optional, more defensible version** that avoids implying this was the first bet (similar length):
"Jerry tried it. Three thousand six hundred dollars in. Six thousand three hundred back."
If you want HL's figures instead, use "$3,400" and add the first loss. Don't mix the two.

## Line 5
> "He and his wife Marge set up a company, sold shares to family and friends, and played every roll-down, until Michigan shut the game down."

**VERDICT: needs fix ("every roll-down" is unverified; sources say 12)**

- CBS, 2019-01-27: "Jerry set up a corporation, G.S. Investment Strategies." … "They invited family and friends to share in their, well, windfall, selling shares in the corporation for $500 apiece." … "By the spring of 2005, Jerry's club stood at 25 members. … They had played Winfall 12 times, winning millions. When Michigan suddenly shut down the game, citing, ironically, lack of sales."
- HL, 2018-03-01: "He gave it an intentionally boring name, GS Investment Strategies LLC, and started selling shares, at $500 apiece, first to the kids and then to friends and colleagues in Evart. Jerry would eventually expand the roster to 25 members". "By the spring of 2005, GS Investment Strategies LLC had played Winfall on 12 different roll-down weeks". "Then, in May 2005, the Michigan Lottery shut down the game with no warning, replacing it with a new one called Classic Lotto 47. Officials claimed that sales of Winfall tickets had been decreasing."
- HL says roll-downs "happened every six weeks or so". No source says the group played every Michigan roll-down. The documented number is 12.
- On the company: both sources say **Jerry** set it up. "He and his wife Marge" is acceptable as a couple's venture, but the strict wording is "Jerry".
- On the Michigan end date: the brief's "2005, after about 12 plays" is confirmed (HL: May 2005; CBS: spring 2005). Wikipedia's Michigan Lottery article says "Introduced on May 15, 2005, Lotto 47 replaced Winfall", but that sentence carries a citation-needed tag. I found no free Michigan Lottery primary record.

**Proposed (27 words vs 25):**
"He and his wife Marge set up a company, sold shares to family and friends, and played twelve roll-downs, until Michigan shut the game down in 2005."

## Line 6
> "Then they found the same game in Massachusetts. For six years, they drove 900 miles each time and bet more than 600,000 dollars a round."

**VERDICT: needs fix ("same game"; "more than $600,000 a round" overgeneralises)**

- **Not the same game.** HL, 2018-03-01, says the plant manager reported that Cash WinFall "appeared to be the same" except that "the jackpot rolled down when it hit $2 million, not $5 million." Tickets also cost $2 instead of $1 (HL: "On a roll-down week, a $2 lottery ticket was worth more than $2"). CBS: "Massachusetts has a game called Cash Winfall", and Jerry needed "Ten minutes" to work it out. Use "a similar game".
- **Six years:** CBS: "a routine they continued for the next six years, driving 900 miles to Massachusetts every time there was a rolldown". Their first Massachusetts play was in August 2005 (HL: "The first time they played Cash WinFall, on August 29") and the last was in January 2012 (HL), so about 6.4 years. HL itself says "Over five years". Keep CBS's figure.
- **900 miles:** CBS: "driving 900 miles to Massachusetts every time there was a rolldown". This is **one way**. HL gives "more than 700 miles" and "the 700 miles from Michigan, cutting across Canada". The figure is fine if attributed to the CBS family.
- **$600,000:** CBS: "Jon Wertheim: So once there was a rolldown, on average, how much were you putting in play? Jerry Selbee: Over $600,000 per play. Seven plays a year." This is Jerry's own estimate. It was not true of every round:
  - HL: the first Massachusetts bet was "$120,000 on 60,000 lottery tickets", later "312,000 individual tickets per roll-down, ultimately going as high as 360,000 tickets—a $720,000 bet on a single drawing."
  - HL's tally of $17.3M played over 43 Massachusetts drawings averages about $400,000 per drawing.
  - GLOBE-AUG12 says they typically aimed for about 312,000 tickets (about $624,000).
  - "More than 600,000 dollars a round" as a flat statement is therefore too strong. "Often" or "up to" fixes it within the CBS family.
- Other brief items: CBS's "Seven plays a year" (HL: "six to nine times per year"). CBS: "Jon Wertheim: $18 million worth of losing tickets. And you have those? Jerry Selbee: Uh-huh just in case we had a physical federal audit."

**Proposed (24 words vs 25):**
"Then they found a similar game in Massachusetts. For six years, they drove 900 miles each time, often betting over 600,000 dollars a round."

## Line 7
> "They weren't alone. A group of MIT students was doing the exact same thing. In 2011, the Boston Globe broke the story, and people assumed the game was rigged."

**VERDICT: needs fix ("exact same thing", "MIT students", "assumed")**

- CBS, 2019-01-27: "The paper's reporting revealed that two groups were dominating Cash Winfall: the Selbee gang from Evart, Michigan, and their competition, a syndicate led by math majors from MIT". Scott Allen: "The guy who started it, he was doing an independent study project as an undergraduate at MIT … recruiting their friends and raising money from backers". "Incredibly, the MIT group bet between $17 and $18 million on Cash Winfall over a seven-year period, earning at least $3.5 million in profits."
- GLOBE-AUG12, 2012-08-01: the MIT group spent "betting between $17 and $18 million over a seven-year period" and "earned at least $3.5 million in profits from 2005 to 2012."
- **Not "exact same":**
  - HL: "Unlike the Selbees, who allowed the computer to pick numbers for them ('Quic Pics'), the MIT students preferred to choose their own".
  - In August 2010 the MIT group bought nearly $1.4M of tickets to force a roll-down itself, which Jerry called unethical. GLOBE-OCT11: "a group led by a 2007 MIT graduate named Yuran Lu … Lu and his MIT classmate James Harvey … bought nearly $1.4 million in tickets". HL: "This was unethical in Jerry's mind".
  - By 2011 the leaders were graduates, not students. The group was Random Strategies LLC (HL: "named Random Strategies LLC, after their dorm").
- **2011 Globe:** HL: "Her story broke on July 31. 'A game with a windfall for a knowing few,' read the headline. The article, co-written with reporter Scott Allen, named Jerry and Marge". Correct.
- **"People assumed the game was rigged":** CBS: "The Boston Globe articles caused a sensation, raising suspicion that the game was rigged." Sullivan (CBS): "the public perception was there must be some kind of organized crime or public corruption". The sources support "suspicion", not "assumed".

**Proposed (29 words, same length):**
"They weren't alone. A group started by MIT students was doing the same thing. In 2011, the Boston Globe broke the story, and many suspected the game was rigged."

## Line 8
> "The state investigated. Its finding: everything was legal. The lottery itself had made 120 million dollars from the game."

**VERDICT: needs fix ("everything was legal" overstates; $120M was to "the state")**

- On the investigation: the Treasurer asked the IG. TREAS: "Treasurer Steven Grossman and the Massachusetts State Lottery asked OIG to conduct a full review". CBS: "It was led by then-state inspector general, Greg Sullivan." HL: "the Massachusetts inspector general released his report on July 27, 2012."
- What the IG cleared (TREAS, July 2012):
  - "OIG found no evidence of any illicit activities or collusion among bettors, Lottery officials, or Lottery agents with respect to Cash WinFall."
  - "No players were disadvantaged by the game – everyone received the same odds and was eligible for the same payouts."
  - "The game's design was not flawed, and it worked as intended."
- What the IG did **not** clear (TREAS): "the syndicates were aided by Lottery retailers – with the assent of some Lottery officials – who bent or broke the rules to allow them to make the high-volume bets. This included such practices as allowing syndicate members to operate Lottery terminals themselves …" and "Suspending the licenses of agents who had inappropriately assisted the syndicates." GLOBE-OCT11: the Lottery "suspended two lottery agents who broke the rules by letting Selbee and his wife Marge operate ticket terminals."
- So "everything was legal" is too broad: lottery rules were broken by retailers. "The betting was legal" is accurate. CBS (Sullivan): "these math-nerd geniuses had found a way legally to win a state lottery". CBS: "The investigation found no one's odds of winning was affected by high-volume betting."
- **$120M:** CBS (Sullivan): "And the state got very rich. The state made $120 million." HL: "Over seven and a half years, Cash WinFall had pumped nearly $120 million into state coffers". TREAS: profits "were returned to the Commonwealth's General Fund". Say "the state", which matches CBS's wording.

**Proposed (21 words vs 19):**
"The state investigated. Its finding: the betting was legal, and no one's odds were hurt. The state made 120 million dollars from the game."

## Line 9
> "In the end, the Selbees' company won more than 26 million dollars, and kept nearly 8 million in profit before tax. They spent it fixing up their home and paying for their grandchildren's education."

**VERDICT: needs fix (second sentence; "kept")**

- CBS, 2019-01-27: "In total, their unlikely homegrown company grossed more than $26 million from nine years of playing the lottery." "They made nearly $8 million in profit before taxes. Back in Evart … the Selbees put their winnings to practical use, renovating their home and helping their six kids, 14 grandkids and 10 great-grandchildren pay for their education."
- HL, 2018-03-01: "grossed nearly $27 million" and "They'd netted $7.75 million in profit before taxes, distributed among the players in GS Investment Strategies LLC." HL's Final Tally: Michigan $1.8M played, $2.65M won; Massachusetts $17.3M played, $24.2M won (total about $26.85M). This is consistent with CBS's "more than $26 million".
- The figures are correct (CBS family). But:
  - "Kept" implies the Selbees kept about $8M. The profit was split among the company's members (25 by 2005; GLOBE-OCT11 says "32-member group" in 2011). "Made" is CBS's word.
  - "They spent it fixing up their home and paying for their grandchildren's education" overstates and narrows the claim. CBS says "helping" with education for kids, grandkids **and** great-grandkids. HL also lists a truck, a camping trailer, silver and gold coins, a savings account, and lending to home builders.

**Proposed (34 words, same length):**
"In the end, the Selbees' company won more than 26 million dollars, and made nearly 8 million in profit before tax. They used theirs to fix up their home and help their family pay for school."

## Line 10
> "Sometimes the edge isn't luck. It's reading the rules."

**VERDICT: no factual claim (editorial close). Acceptable.**
It is neutral and isn't advice. One caveat: chance still played a part (HL: "The group lost money in only three drawings"), which "sometimes" already allows for. No change needed.

## Rev 2 (2026-11-26) — the WinFall brochure's print
- **Michigan WinFall was pick 6 of 49** (launched January 2003, replacing Michigan Millions' 51-number field; jackpot odds "1 in 14 million").
  Source: web search summaries of the Michigan Bureau of State Lottery 2003 Annual Report
  (https://www.michigan.gov/documents/BSL-L-AR2003_133757_7.pdf) and the Lottery's announcement as reported by the Las Vegas Sun /
  AP, 2002-12-12 (https://lasvegassun.com/news/2002/dec/12/lottery-announces-new-game/). Both pages refused a direct fetch (403 / 402),
  so this is secondary-level confidence. The brochure's odds are COMPUTED from 6 of 49 (1 in 13,983,816 for the jackpot).
- Lower-tier prize AMOUNTS ($2,500 / $100 / $5 in the summaries) are NOT printed: no primary source could be read. The brochure says
  "set by the game rules" and makes no pari-mutuel claim.
