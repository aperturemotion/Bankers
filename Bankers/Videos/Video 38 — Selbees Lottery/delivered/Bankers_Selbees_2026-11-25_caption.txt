A retired couple from a town of about 1,900 people beat the lottery, legally, for nine years.

In 2003, Jerry Selbee, a retired convenience-store owner in Evart, Michigan, read the brochure for a new Michigan Lottery game, Winfall. He spotted the catch in about three minutes: when the jackpot reached $5 million and nobody matched all six numbers, the money "rolled down" to the players who matched three, four or five. On those draws, a big enough pile of tickets put the math on the buyer's side. His first serious try: about $3,600 of tickets, about $6,300 back.

He and his wife Marge formed a company with family and friends and played 12 roll-downs before Michigan replaced the game in 2005. Then they found a similar game, Massachusetts' Cash WinFall, and for about six years drove some 900 miles a trip, often betting more than $600,000 a round. A group started by MIT students was playing it too. In 2011 the Boston Globe reported on the big bettors, and many suspected the game was rigged.

The state's Inspector General investigated and found the high-volume betting legal: no illicit activity, and no other player's odds were hurt. The state itself made about $120 million from the game. Over nine years the Selbees' company won more than $26 million, about $8 million of it profit before tax, shared among its members.

To be fair, the investigation also found that some lottery retailers bent the rules to sell the big bettors' tickets, and the games were shut down.

Sometimes the edge isn't luck. It's reading the rules.
