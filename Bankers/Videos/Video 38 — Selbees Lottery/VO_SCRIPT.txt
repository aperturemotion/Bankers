A few drops of blood. That was the promise behind a nine billion dollar company.

In two thousand three, Elizabeth Holmes founded Theranos at nineteen, then dropped out of Stanford.

The pitch: a full set of blood tests from one finger prick, on its Edison machine.

By two thousand fourteen, it was worth nine billion dollars on paper.

Two former secretaries of state sat on its board.

Walgreens offered its tests in about forty stores.

But most tests ran on other companies' machines, not the Edison.

One whistleblower was Tyler Shultz, a board member's grandson.

In October two thousand fifteen, the Wall Street Journal exposed it.

Theranos voided two years of Edison results. Walgreens walked away. In two thousand eighteen, it shut down.

In January two thousand twenty-two, Holmes was found guilty of defrauding investors. Her sentence: eleven years and three months, later cut by one year.

To be fair, she testified that she believed in the technology.

And she was cleared of defrauding patients.

The lesson: famous names are not proof.

At its peak, Theranos had published no peer-reviewed data for outside scientists to check.
