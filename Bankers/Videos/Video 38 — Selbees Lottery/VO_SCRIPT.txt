A retired couple from a town of 1,900 people beat the lottery. Legally. For nine years.
In 2003, Jerry Selbee, who had just sold his convenience store in Evart, Michigan, picked up a brochure for a new game called Winfall. It took him three minutes to find the flaw.
When the jackpot reached five million dollars and nobody won it, the money didn't roll over. It rolled down, to everyone who matched three, four or five numbers. On those days, if you bought enough tickets, the math was on your side.
Jerry tested it. Three thousand six hundred dollars in. Six thousand three hundred back.
He and his wife Marge set up a company, sold shares to family and friends, and played every roll-down, until Michigan shut the game down.
Then they found the same game in Massachusetts. For six years, they drove 900 miles each time and bet more than 600,000 dollars a round.
They weren't alone. A group of MIT students was doing the exact same thing. In 2011, the Boston Globe broke the story, and people assumed the game was rigged.
The state investigated. Its finding: everything was legal. The lottery itself had made 120 million dollars from the game.
In the end, the Selbees' company won more than 26 million dollars, and kept nearly 8 million in profit before tax. They spent it fixing up their home and paying for their grandchildren's education.
Sometimes the edge isn't luck. It's reading the rules.
