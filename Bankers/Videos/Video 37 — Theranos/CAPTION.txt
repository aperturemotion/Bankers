A few drops of blood. That was the promise behind a $9 billion company.

In 2003, Elizabeth Holmes founded Theranos at 19, then dropped out of Stanford. The pitch: a full set of blood tests from one finger prick, on its Edison machine. By 2014 it was valued at about $9 billion, with two former US secretaries of state on its board and Theranos centers in about 40 Walgreens stores.

But most of its tests ran on other companies' machines. The SEC later said its own analyzer ran only about 12 of the more than 200 tests it offered. One whistleblower was Tyler Shultz, the grandson of board member George Shultz. On October 15, 2015, the Wall Street Journal reported the problems.

In 2016, Theranos voided two years of Edison results and Walgreens ended the partnership. The company dissolved in September 2018. In January 2022, a jury found Holmes guilty on four counts of defrauding investors. She was sentenced to 11 years and 3 months, a term a judge cut by 12 months in March 2026.

To be fair, Holmes testified that she believed in the technology, and the jury acquitted her of the charges of defrauding patients.

The lesson: famous names are not proof. At its peak, Theranos had published no peer-reviewed data for outside scientists to check.
