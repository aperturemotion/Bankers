When a retired janitor in Vermont died in twenty fourteen, his will revealed he was worth almost eight million dollars.

Ronald Read grew up poor on a Vermont farm and was the first in his family to finish high school.

After the war, he pumped gas for about twenty-five years, then worked as a part-time janitor at JCPenney for seventeen more.

He drove a second-hand Toyota Yaris, held his coat together with a safety pin, and chopped his own firewood into his nineties. A stranger once paid for his meal, thinking he couldn't afford it.

What nobody knew: for decades, he'd quietly bought shares in companies he understood, and held on to them.

When he died, he owned at least ninety-five stocks. The certificates in his safe deposit box stood about five inches high.

Not every pick worked. He held Lehman Brothers when it collapsed in two thousand eight. It was one stock out of dozens.

Then came the will: four point eight million dollars to the local hospital, one point two million to the library. Twice its yearly budget.

In twenty twenty-three, the hospital opened a new wing, partly paid for by his gift: the Ronald Read Pavilion.

He never earned a big salary. He just gave his money time.
