Gold dust, sprinkled into rock samples, built a C$6 billion company.

In 1993, a small Calgary miner called Bre-X bought the Busang gold project in Borneo, Indonesia. By February 1997, it claimed 71 million ounces of gold, and its market value had reached about C$6 billion. Pension funds piled in.

In March 1997, its partner Freeport-McMoRan drilled its own test holes. On March 19, chief geologist Michael de Guzman fell from a helicopter. A week later, Freeport reported "insignificant amounts of gold."

On May 4, 1997, independent auditors Strathcona Minerals confirmed that the core samples had been salted with outside gold. Two days later, Bre-X shares sank to 6 cents. About 40,000 shareholders were nearly wiped out, and the Ontario Teachers' Pension Plan alone lost C$100 million.

To be fair, no one was ever convicted. Exploration chief John Felderhof sold about C$84 million of stock in 1996 and was found not guilty of insider trading on July 31, 2007.

The lesson: a company's own results aren't proof. Since 2001, Canada's NI 43-101 rule requires a qualified person behind every mining disclosure, and an independent one in key cases.
