Gold dust, sprinkled into rock samples. That's all it took to build a six billion dollar company.

In nineteen ninety-three, a tiny Calgary miner called Bre-X bought a jungle gold project in Borneo, Indonesia. By nineteen ninety-seven, it claimed seventy-one million ounces.

The stock went from pennies to a six billion dollar company. Pension funds piled in.

That March, a partner drilled its own test holes beside Bre-X's.

On March nineteenth, chief geologist Michael de Guzman fell from a helicopter. A week later, the partner reported insignificant amounts of gold.

On May fourth, independent auditors confirmed it: the core samples had been salted with outside gold.

Two days later, the shares sank to six cents. About forty thousand shareholders were nearly wiped out. The Ontario Teachers' Pension Plan alone lost a hundred million dollars.

To be fair, no one was ever convicted. Exploration chief John Felderhof sold eighty-four million dollars of stock in nineteen ninety-six, and was found not guilty of insider trading.

The lesson: a company's own results aren't proof. Since two thousand one, Canadian miners need a qualified expert's sign-off, and often an independent one.
